# Vision Claw Development Plan

# Goal

Turn Vision Claw from a browser demo into a practical camera-based cursor control tool for people whose hands are occupied or gloved. Over approximately six months, the focus will be improving tracking, enabling control across desktop applications, and testing whether it helps healthcare professionals, laboratory technicians, and mechanical engineers.

# Milestones and timeline

- **Month 1 — Establish a baseline and seek advice.** Measure cursor accuracy, jitter, response time, tracking loss, and calibration time with the current setup. Speak with potential academic advisers, including Professor Usevich, and seek introductions to BYU researchers working on drone motion tracking. Use their feedback to refine camera placement, calibration, and testing.

- **Months 1–2 — Evaluate camera improvements.** Compare the current cameras with better-quality cameras and test three or more camera views. Check whether added views improve accuracy and reduce tracking loss when a hand or object blocks a camera. Weigh any gains against cost, processing demands, and setup complexity before selecting the hardware.

- **Months 1–3 — Pursue NSF funding.** With Alex Dalpe’s help, identify a suitable National Science Foundation grant opportunity, confirm eligibility and deadlines, and prepare an application covering camera improvements, prototype development, and user testing. Aim to submit within this period, subject to the program’s application schedule.

- **Months 2–3 — Move beyond the browser.** Enable the cursor to move and click across desktop applications on an initial supported operating system. Include a simple way to pause control and recover from lost tracking. Milestone: demonstrate a complete task in a desktop application using Vision Claw.

- **Months 3–4 — Test with intended users.** Recruit a small initial group, aiming for 2–3 people from each profession. Observe representative tasks such as navigating a mock healthcare record, reviewing laboratory information, and manipulating a CAD model. Use simulated tasks without patient data. Record task completion time, errors, comfort, and user feedback.

- **Months 5–6 — Refine and prepare a focused pilot.** Address the most common failures, simplify setup, and repeat the key tests. Choose the strongest initial use case and prepare a repeatable demonstration, recommended hardware setup, and plan for a larger pilot.

# People and resources

Development will need computer vision and desktop software expertise, academic advice on motion tracking and calibration, and access to healthcare professionals, lab technicians, and mechanical engineers for feedback. Seek Alex Dalpe’s guidance on the NSF application, project scope, and budget. Resources include loaned or purchased camera options, mounts, calibration targets, a suitable computer, and access to representative workstations and software. Start with borrowed equipment where possible; use the camera comparison to guide a modest hardware and participant-testing budget.

# Expected outcome

By the end of six months, aim to have a desktop-capable prototype, evidence showing whether better cameras and three-or-more-camera setups are worthwhile, and feedback from all three user groups. Success means reliable task completion with manageable setup and a clear choice of the first workflow to pursue. The schedule is an initial estimate and depends on development progress and participant access.
